# The apps doubled. The appetite didn't.

New iOS apps roughly doubled in about a year. A new NBER paper tracked it: 30,000 to 50,000 a month from 2023 into early 2025, then a climb to around 100,000 a month by April 2026. Appfigures, counting independently, lands in the same place: 557,000 new App Store apps last year, the biggest year since 2016, with iOS releases up another 80% in early 2026. Agentic coding made building nearly free, so people build. That part isn't surprising. What got me was the usage side. Taken together, all those new apps weren't getting used any more than before. Supply went vertical. Demand just sat there.

My first instinct was that this is a choice problem. There's a famous study where a shop put out 24 jams to taste instead of 6, pulled a bigger crowd, and then sold to 3% of them instead of 30%. Pile up the shelf and people walk away with nothing. The digital version writes itself: when the store already holds five million apps and a thousand more showed up this week, are you really going to go looking? An Alibaba experiment on 1.6 million shoppers showed the same thing: purchases peaked at two recommendations and fell after that, and about two-thirds of the lost sales came from people who never started searching at all. So the hunch was that past a certain size, the pile itself becomes the problem. Make the shelf big enough and people just stop reaching for anything new. And if nobody wants to dig through it, they fall back on the apps they already trust. The everything-app. WeChat logic.

Except the data fights me on that. The jam study turns out to be a bit of an outlier. The biggest meta-analysis on choice overload, around 50 experiments and 5,000 people, puts the average effect at basically zero. And people are clearly not too overwhelmed to try new things: time spent in AI apps hit 48 billion hours last year, more than triple the year before, and ChatGPT's usage was up over 400%. That's not a tired consumer. The paper that started all this (Demirer and co, worth a read) doesn't even lead with my theory. Its version is duller and probably righter: the amount of code people write jumped by as much as 180%, but shipping a finished app only sped up about 30%, because the hard part was never the code. So a lot of those new apps are just half-built things nobody was ever going to open. You don't need my overwhelmed-consumer theory to explain a flat line. The boring supply-side story already does.

So the "everyone flees to a WeChat for the West" ending falls apart too. Western super-apps keep dying. Meta tried it, X is trying it, and something like 65% of Americans say they don't even want one. But I don't think the consolidation instinct was wrong, just the shape of it. Look at where the attention actually went. OpenAI and DeepSeek alone took about half of all AI app downloads, and three apps now eat 90% of the time people spend with AI assistants. Nobody's building a Western WeChat. People are just quietly drifting back into the few names they already trust. Turns out the scarce thing was never apps. It was a reason to open a new one.
