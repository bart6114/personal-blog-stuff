Coding agents have got much better at working through longer tasks over the past three months. I'm increasingly convinced that a significant number of B2B software products, including fairly large ones, will lose deals to customers building for themselves. Say a company pays for an accounts payable system but mostly uses it to collect invoices, route approvals, schedule payments and update its ERP. It only needs to build the part it uses, which is a much smaller job than recreating the entire product.

There's an early sign of the shift: in [McKinsey's 2026 survey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai), 32% of respondents said their organization had passed on at least one software product or feature because it could build the functionality with agentic coding tools. That figure doesn't tell us how much spending has moved. The company in my example might build its invoice workflow and still buy the payment rails underneath it. Running those rails means dealing with compliance, banks and the responsibility for moving money. Writing the code was never the whole job.

Getting that invoice workflow into production still means connecting it to the ERP, handling exceptions, keeping an audit trail and taking the call when it breaks. My guess is that many enterprises buy complete AI solutions today because they lack people with that experience. Their teams will learn to build with agents, while the tools and ways of using them keep changing. A service provider working across customers gets more chances to learn what works in production and what needs revisiting. The good ones can arrive with a strong opinion about how to get the customer's system running and keep it useful. That's where I expect demand for their experience to grow.

There is a catch for the provider. If a small team can get the invoice workflow running in months, there are fewer hours to bill than in a large, multiyear implementation. Some firms will do well with shorter projects and ongoing support. The version I find most interesting leaves software running in the customer's infrastructure: tools for deploying agents, sandboxing them, checking their work and monitoring them. The customer owns its workflow and data. The provider keeps improving those tools and charges for licenses, support and maintenance. Each new deployment should improve the shared tools, even though some integration work will always be specific to the customer.

[Wonderful](https://www.wonderful.ai/deployment) combines teams working inside enterprises with a reusable AI platform. It's one example of the shape, not proof that every service company should do this. By 2027, I expect more enterprises to own workflows built with outside experts and pay those experts to keep the tools behind them current. I'd want to know what happens six months after launch. Does the workflow still work, and does the customer call the same team when the next problem turns up? That's the kind of service company I'd bet on.
