Over the last few months, I’ve been doing some soul-searching about what comes after exiting a company. I want to build again, and I have a few ideas that still need to crystallize a bit. But one thing I’ve become fairly sure about is that I want the next company to be stakeholder-centric at its core.

That thinking has taken shape through my experiences over the years, both building Dataroots and through the advisory work I’ve been doing since then.

## Looking back at Dataroots

Before our acquisition, we described our priorities in some of our yearly team updates as customer experience first, employee experience second, and financial health as a constraint. We wanted to do excellent work for customers, with a team that was happy doing it, while keeping the company financially healthy.

Dataroots then became part of a large international group with substantial external capital behind it. From a growth perspective, it’s an impressive story. That capital helped make its rapid international expansion possible. It also made me think more about how ownership shapes the priorities of a business.

When investment funds become significant owners, they get a significant say. Those funds are investing money on behalf of other people, who expect a return. Someone whose investment ends up partly in a specific company is several steps removed from its customers and team. Their relationship with the company is primarily financial. I invest for a return too, and I think that’s perfectly fair.

Most of the time, I think the interests of customers, the team, and owners line up quite well. The difficult decisions are the ones where investing in customers or the team means giving up some financial return today. I might believe that investment will produce a better return later, but how much better, and how much later, is often hard to say.

After the acquisition, those choices needed agreement in a wider context. I was accountable to a large international organisation, which was accountable to a fund, which in turn was accountable to its investors. An investment with an immediate cost and a less certain future benefit needed to make sense across those different perspectives.

That experience helped me understand what I want for my next company. I’d like us to agree from the start on how we approach investments in customers and the team, including when the financial payoff is difficult to predict. I want those priorities reflected in how the company is owned and governed.

## What I mean by stakeholder-centric

Shareholders are the owners of a company. Stakeholders are the people with a stake in how it operates, including its customers, employees, and owners. Those are the three groups I’m focusing on here, although a business affects other people too.

A shareholder-centric company puts the financial interests of its owners first. It can look after customers and employees very well, and invest patiently for the long term. In the end, the ultimate test for a shareholder-centric company is whether those decisions have served to maximize the owners’ returns.

In a stakeholder-centric company, customers and employees have interests that matter in their own right, alongside those of the owners. Their success and wellbeing are part of what the company is trying to achieve. There is a reason to invest in them even when we can’t put a confident number or date on the financial payoff. Sometimes that may also mean accepting a smaller return.

For me, that means building a financially healthy company around customer success, with an excellent and happy team. The money we earn should allow us to keep investing in both and build a buffer for the future, while also providing a return for the owners.

## For the next company

I have the luxury of thinking this through before starting again. I want to use that freedom to build a company that aligns as closely as possible with my values and beliefs. For me, that means a stakeholder-centric organisation.<sup><a href="#note-personal-fit" id="ref-personal-fit" data-footnote-ref aria-label="Footnote 1: personal fit" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>

Those priorities that we communicated back in the day in our team updates still fit what I want to build. Helping customers succeed requires an excellent team that cares about the work. I want to invest in those people, pay them well, and give them room to grow. I want them to feel proud to be part of the team because they’re working with strong colleagues and building excellent products or services together. I want to build a company people want to be part of.

I strongly believe that when you consistently help customers succeed and have an excellent team behind that work, you can charge premium prices for your products or services. Get that foundation right, and I expect strong financial results to follow. That’s how I want to approach building the business: organise around doing excellent work for customers with a team that thrives, and earn our return through that.
