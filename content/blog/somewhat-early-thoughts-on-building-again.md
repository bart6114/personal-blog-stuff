I want to build again. I have a few ideas that still need to crystallize a bit, but I’ve become clearer about the kind of company I want to build. The harder part is thinking through what its priorities would mean when there’s a cost to sticking to them.

My starting point is to help customers succeed, with an excellent and happy team, while keeping the company financially healthy. Helping customers requires people who care about the work. I want to pay them well and give them room to grow, working with strong colleagues on products or services they’re proud of.

I strongly believe that consistently helping customers succeed with a team like that gives you a basis for charging premium prices. Get that foundation right, and I expect strong financial results to follow. Most of the time, I think the interests of customers, the team, and owners line up quite well.

The difficult decisions are the ones where investing in customers or the team means giving up some financial return today. I might believe that investment will produce a better return later, but how much better, and how much later, is often hard to say. And sometimes it may simply mean accepting a smaller return.

I want there to be room for those decisions too. Paying people well and helping customers succeed are things I want the company to achieve even when I can’t make a convincing case that they’ll maximise the owners’ returns.

That’s what I mean by a stakeholder-centric company. The stakeholders I’m focusing on here are customers, employees, and owners, though a business affects other people too. Their success and wellbeing matter in their own right.

A company focused on shareholder returns can look after customers and employees very well, and invest patiently for the long term. The distinction I care about is what ultimately justifies those decisions: whether they maximise the owners’ returns, or whether the benefit to customers and the team can be reason enough. I invest for a return too, and I think that’s perfectly fair.

There’s still a financial constraint. The company needs to earn enough to keep operating, invest, build a buffer for the future, and provide a return for its owners. That limits what it can afford to do for customers and the team. Agreeing that their interests matter won’t make every decision easy.

I have the luxury of thinking this through before starting again. I’m definitely not claiming this is a better way for everyone to build a company. It’s simply the way I want to build mine.

That makes ownership part of what I need to think through now. I’d like us to agree from the start on how we approach investments in customers and the team, including when the financial payoff is uncertain or the return may be smaller. I want those priorities reflected in who gets to make those decisions and what they’re accountable for.
