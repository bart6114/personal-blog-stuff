# Fishy Operations voice evidence

Use this reference to calibrate substantial Bart-authored writing. It records recurring choices from 16 published posts in the Fishy Operations archive, dated September 2014 through January 2018.

## Corpus boundaries

The source archive was `Bart6114-fishyoperations-blog-877775125dc2.zip`. The analysis included the 16 published posts written by Bart and excluded:

- five posts whose front matter names Iñaki Ucar as author;
- three unpublished drafts;
- code, console output, and embedded scripts when judging prose rhythm.

This is mostly an older technical-blog corpus. Treat repeated rhetorical choices as evidence of voice. Do not make its era-specific Markdown, package-release format, smileys, or grammar mistakes compulsory.

## What repeats

| Observed choice | Representative evidence | Writing instruction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A real need comes before the tool | simmer “grew out of a personal need”; infuser started because a limitation “irked me so much” | Name the friction or use case before describing features. |
| The object appears early | Posts often begin by naming the package, update, experiment, or decision | Avoid a broad preamble. Put the reader in the actual piece immediately. |
| Claims stay bounded | Early releases are called “alpha,” “very early,” or a “quick implementation” | State maturity and limits without defensive ceremony. |
| Examples do the persuading | Technical posts move quickly into a small example, output, or screenshot | Replace adjectives with something the reader can inspect. |
| The reader is treated as capable | Explanations are brief and assume curiosity rather than ignorance | Supply the missing context, not a textbook chapter. |
| Personal agency remains visible | “I started tweaking,” “I simply cannot find the time or interest anymore” | Use `I` for Bart's motives and decisions. Do not depersonalize them. |
| Humor points inward or at the thing | The Jug name is treated as a slightly silly joke; asides soften technical prose | Keep humor light, sparse, and non-performative. |
| Endings are functional | Posts finish with a link, request for specific feedback, status note, or clean stop | Do not bolt on a moral or summary. |

## The characteristic movement

Many posts follow a loose sequence:

1. Here is the need, change, or experiment.
2. Here is the smallest useful demonstration.
3. Here is what it means and where it does not apply.
4. Here is where to try it, read more, or respond.

This is a tendency, not a template. A reflective piece may spend more time on the decision. A short update may stop after the second step.

## Tone in practice

### Practical curiosity

Bart often follows a thought because he wants to know whether it works. The neural-network compensation post begins with an everyday pricing anecdote, turns it into a small experiment, and explicitly warns readers not to treat it as a correct training method. Preserve that mix of play and epistemic caution.

### Modest authority

The prose is technically competent without trying to sound definitive. Phrases such as “very basic templating engine,” “quick implementation,” and “minimum amount of coding” lower the temperature. Use this kind of understatement only when it is accurate; do not turn it into a verbal tic.

### Honest project ownership

The last Jug post is useful evidence beyond tutorials. It states the decision immediately, ties the project to a concrete past need, credits the person who improved simmer, and admits that time and interest moved elsewhere. It does not manufacture a victory out of stopping.

### Conversational joins

Questions and phrases such as “So, how does this work?”, “Let's say,” and “Okay, okay” sometimes connect sections. Their function is to move the thought along. Use a conversational transition only where spoken prose genuinely needs one.

## Stable traits versus old surface habits

Keep:

- direct entry;
- personal motivation where relevant;
- concrete demonstration;
- specific caveats;
- short, purposeful paragraphs;
- understated humor;
- a clean or practical ending.

Modernize or omit:

- spelling and grammar errors;
- automatic smileys;
- generic “feedback welcome” endings;
- old package-installation boilerplate;
- dense release-note lists when a changelog link would work better;
- dated link and code formatting.

## Calibration questions

When a draft feels wrong, ask:

- What concrete need, observation, or decision is hiding behind the introduction?
- Which claim could become an example?
- Which limitation would Bart volunteer before a reader has to discover it?
- Is the humor attached to something real, or was it added to imitate personality?
- Can the final paragraph simply stop one paragraph earlier?
